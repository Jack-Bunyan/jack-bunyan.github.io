- Somatostatin (SST) is a cyclic peptide hormone and is involved in the inhibition of numerous metabolic processes, including:
    - endocrine secretions (e.g., growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, and gastrin).
- It is also involved in modulating exocrine secretions (e.g., gastric acid and pancreatic enzymes).
- All over the body, there are five different subtypes of somatostatin receptors (SSTR):
    - **SSTR1** - involved in the antisecretory effects of growth hormone, prolactin and calcitonin
    - **SSTR2** - inhibits the secretion of growth hormone and adrenocorticotropin (hormone that stimulates the adrenal glands), glucagon, insulin, interferon-γ (protein produced by immune cells), and stomach acid interferon-γ (protein produced by immune cells), and stomach acid
    - **SSTR3** - reduces cell proliferation and causes cell apoptosis
    - **SSTR4 **_- functions are not yet well defined_
    - **SSTR5** - inhibits the secretion of growth hormone and adrenocorticotropin (hormone that stimulates the adrenal glands), glucagon, insulin, and inhibits secretion of amylase

# Anatomy and Physiology
---
## Anatomy

![[neuroendocrine system  image.png]]
*Main parts of the neuroendocrine system ([Source](https://cancer.ca/en/cancer-information/cancer-types/neuroendocrine-tumours/what-are-neuroendocrine-tumours/the-neuroendocrine-system))*

- The neuroendocrine system is made up of neuroendocrine cells.
	- these are spread throughout the body
	- they are mainly responsible for hormone production
		- controlled by signals produced by the nervous system
	- these hormones control many body functions
- Neuroendocrine cells are found in almost every organ in the body.

- But they are mainly found in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.
	- including small intestine, rectum, stomach, colon, oesophagus and appendix
	- as well as the gallbladder, pancreas, thyroid, lungs, pituitary gland, parathyroid glands, inner layer of adrenal gland

## Physiology

- Neuroendocrine cells make and release hormones and peptides in response to neurological or chemical signals.
- These hormones travel through the bloodstream and travel to the target cells.
- The hormones then attach to specific receptors on these target cells, causing changes in the cells and their function.
- They are responsible in the control of the following:
    - release of digestive enzymes to break down food
    - speed of food moving through GI tract
    - air and blood flow through the lungs
    - blood pressure and heart rate
    - amount of glucose in blood
    - bone and muscle growth and development

## Patient Preparation (to finish)
---

**The ARSAC licence holder must approve the stopping of any medications but the following is recommended**

- Stop short acting somatostatin analogues for 3 days prior to the start of the examination
- Stop lanreotide for at least 3 weeks prior to the start of the examination
- Stop octreotide for at least 5 weeks prior to the start of the examination
- Other long acting formulations to be stopped for 4-6 weeks as for In111 Octreotide
    - However, it should be noted that these recommendations are given on empirical grounds, the absolute need for such measure has not been demonstrated. In some patients the withdrawal of therapy might be not tolerated and may cause rebound effects. This is notably the case in insulinoma patients, where the danger of sudden hypoglycaemia must be considered, and in patients suffering from the carcinoid syndrome
- Patient should be prescribed a laxative to be taken on the day before injection and and the day before each imaging session (Senna, 2×7.5mg tablets nocté). Laxatives should not be used in patients with insulinomas or with active diarrhoea
- Ask the patient to empty their bladder prior to imaging. Ask incontinent patients to change to a clean pad/empty any catheter bags. Position any catheter bag away from the legs whilst imaging.

## Assessment of Neuroendocrine Tumours (NETs)

### What is it?

> _A neuroendocrine tumour is a rare tumour that can develop in many different organs of the body. It affects the cells that release hormones into the bloodstream (neuroendocrine cells).

![[NET location.png]]
*Common locations for NETs within the thorax/abdomen/pelvis (TAP).*

- ~50% of NETs start in the digestive system.
- ~20% of NETs start in the lung.
- All NETs are malignant by definition.
    - these are slower growing and they are classed as being well differentiated
        - these are easily identified on scans as they are SSTR dense
    - if they grow fast and are poorly differentiated then they tend to be called neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs)
- NETs are classified as a “_diverse group of epithelial neoplasms_”.
    - **epithelial** - layers of cells that line hollow organs and glands ([Source](https://app.notion.com/p/Somatostatin-Octreotide-Receptor-Imaging-bc397eaa8bdb49ac9532ac0fb6a18837?pvs=21))
    - **neoplasms** - an abnormal growth of tissue ([Source](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22319-malignant-neoplasm))
        - neoplasms can be split into malignant or benign
        - malignant neoplasms include:
            - _carcinomas_ - ~90% of cancer cases, originating in epithelial cells (NETs)
            - _sarcomas_ - in connective tissue (like bone, cartilage, muscle, tendons and fat)
            - _myelomas -_ forms in plasma cells of bone marrow
            - _leukemias -_ known as blood cancer, this is cancer of bone marrow
            - _lymphomas -_ develops in glands or nodes of lymphatic system
