title: On X-Ray Tubes
---
X-ray tubes are designed to produce a stream of x-ray photons.  This is done by accelerating electrons across a potential into a metal target, where they decelerate producing [[On Particle Interactions#Radiative Losses|Bremstrahlung]] radiation.

![[x-ray diagram self on phone.png|371]]![[x-ray tube glass bulb.png|316]]

These are used throughout medical physics in [[Planar X-ray]], [[CT]], [[CT#CBCT|CBCT]], [[Mammography]],and [[Contact Radiotherapy]], producing a spectrum of X-rays which has a maximum energy equal to the voltage across the tube.  This spectrum is produced by a combination of the bremsstrahlung radiation, and the [[On Particle Interactions#Photo-electric effect|Characteristic]] X-rays associated with that material.

The filament is contained within the focusing cup which makes up the cathode, when a current is passed through and the wire and heats it up and thermionic [emission occurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermionic_emission) releasing electrons into the vacuum.  This is held inside the focusing cup which makes the focal point smaller increasing [[Resolution]].
![[electron filament kv tube.png]]

These electrons are then accelerated across the vacuum using a voltage which depends on the application, typically it is around 100kV, however for mammography much lower voltages around 35kV are used, and contact radiotherapy uses 50kV.

The accelerated electrons then collide with the anode.  Tungsten is a common material used for x-ray tubes as a result of its high atomic number making x-ray production efficient; high melting point making it capable of handling long exposures; and characteristic x-rays being within the useful region of the energy spectrum.  Some systems will use a rotating design for the anode to dissipate the heat energy across the component.
