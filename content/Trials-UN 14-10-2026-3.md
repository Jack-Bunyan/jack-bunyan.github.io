Radiotherapy is a complex process with many steps, unlike many drug trials you don't just switch one option with another, this can make the design of clinical trials quite complex.  Cannot make the assumption that a treatment plan that appears to be better actually is.  It is rare that a radiotherapy trial will involve a placebo, use of the standard of care treatment methodology is more typical, comparing the idea with an already established method.  The purpose of randomisation is to make comparison more fair, using both a control arm and an experimental arm.  Typical trials will be double blind, this is very difficult to ensure is the case in radiotherapy trials, since each treatment is bespoke.

These difficulties to double-blind radiotherapy trials makes the use of quantative measures of outcomes far more important, in an effort to reduce any bias that could occur in the interpretation of results.  

>Randomisation is only ethical when genuine uncertainty remains


All trials will start with some sort of new idea, this could be [[Dose]] escalation, dose reduction, hypofractionation, a new technology, or new planning methods.  It then needs to be hypothesised what sort of benefits this will provide to the patients.  The question should be precise, who is it for, what is the change, how will it help, how will this be measured?

Studies are either observational or interventional
Observational
- Looking backwards on what has already been done, big data?
Interventional
- Researchers assign an intervention according to a new idea

There are many affects that could be measured
- tumour control
- toxicity
- patient outcomes
- surrogate outcomes

The number of trial members should be established such that the confidence in the result is high without wasting resources.  If performing a multi-centre trial establishing key criteria as to the delivery of the treatment is very important, as otherwise the variation between groups of the same arm may be larger than the variation within an arm.  Clear instructions, techniques and quality assurance.

# What is better?

- Superiority, do we have a better way of treating the patient and ensuring their quality of life
- equivalent, this can be a great result if looking to reduce resources, either cost or number of fractions
- non-inferiority

This should be considered only while also considering the effect size, confidence interval, and clinical threshold.  Further, consider whether not only is the analysis performed correct considering the trial itself and their controls, but also the work done; what is the purpose of a trial with incredibly beneficial effects if it requires the patient be treated in Dull, Scotland on a full moon and a birthday in March?
